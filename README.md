## **Golay's Merit Factor Problem with Cross-Entropy Method (CEM)**

Let $N$ be a positive integer and $\mathcal{A}_N$ be the set of all sequence $A := (a_j)_{j=0}^{N-1}$ with $a_j \in \{-1, 1\}$ for every $0 \le j \le N-1$. Consider the aperiodic autocorrelation coefficients $c_k$ for $0 \le k \le N-1$ defined as

$$c_A(k) = \sum_{j= 0}^{N - k -1}a_j a_{j+k}.$$

Then, define the Golay's Merit Factor

$$F(A) := \frac{N^2}{2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}c_A^2(k)}$$

and denote $F_N := \max_{A \in \mathcal{A}_N} F(A)$.

The Merit Factor Problem is to determine the value $F := \limsup_{N \to \infty}F(A_N).$

Golay conjectured in 1982 that $F \approx 12.32$.

### **1. Relationship between $\mathcal{A}_n$ and the $L^4$ norms relative to $L^2$ of polynomials**

Let $P(z) = a_{N-1}z^{N-1} + a_{N-2}z^{N-2} + \cdots + a_1 z + a_0$ associated with $A \in \mathcal{A}_N$. On the unit circle, $z = e^{i\theta}$ with its conjugate $\overline{z} = z^{-1}$. Then, observe that

$$|P(z)|^2 = P(z)P(\overline{z}) = \sum_{k = 0}^{N-1}c_A(k)(z^k + z^{-k}) = 2(n + \sum_{k = 1}^{N-1}c_A(k)\cos(k\theta)).$$

By Parseval, the $L^2$ norm

$$\lVert P \rVert_2 = \left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|P(e^{i\theta})|^2 d\theta\right)^{1/2} = \sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}}a_j^2 = \sqrt{N},$$

and the $L^4$ norm $\lVert P \rVert_4$ satisfies

$$\lVert P \rVert_4^4 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|P(e^{i\theta})|^4 d\theta = N^2 + 2\sum_{k = 1}^{N-1}c_A^2(k).$$

Hence, we have

$$\left(\frac{\lVert P \rVert_4}{\lVert P \rVert_2}\right)^4 = 1 + \frac{1}{F(A)}.$$



### **2. Representations of $A \in \mathcal{A}_N$**

Let $A := (a_j)_{j=0}^{N-1}$ with $a_j \in \{-1, 1\}$ for every $0 \le j \le N-1$. Define the pairwise addition, substraction of two $A, A' \in \mathcal{A}_N$, and a scala multiplication denoted by $A \pm A'$ and $rA$, respectively. Define $A^+ := \frac{1}{2}\left(A + B\right) = (a_j^+)_{j=0}^{N-1}$ and $A^- := -\frac{1}{2}\left(A - B\right)= (a_j^-)_{j=0}^{N-1}$, where $B := (1)_{j=0}^{N-1}$ and $a_j^{\pm} = \pm\frac{a_j \pm 1}{2}$.

Then, all the elements in $A^+$ and $A^-$ are $0$ or $1$. Moreover, $a_j^+ = 1$ (and $0$) if and only if $a_j^- = 0$ (and $1$), repectively. Furthermore, $A = A^+ - A^-$ and $B = A^+ + A^-$.

Define

$$c_0(k) = \sum_{j=0}^{N-k-1}a_j^+a_{j+k}^+, c_1(k) = \sum_{j=0}^{N-k-1}a_j^+a_{j+k}^-, c_2(k) = \sum_{j=0}^{N-k-1}a_j^-a_{j+k}^+, c_3(k) = \sum_{j=0}^{N-k-1}a_j^-a_{j+k}^-.$$

Notice that $c_i(k) \ge 0$ for every $i = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, N.$ Moreover, for each $k$ and $j$, only one of $a_j^{\pm}a_{j+k}^{\pm}$ and $a_j^{\pm}a_{j+k}^{\mp}$ is $1$ and the others are all $0$ from the below table:

| $a_j^+a_{j+k}^+$| $a_j^+a_{j+k}^-$ | $a_j^-a_{j+k}^+$ | $a_j^-a_{j+k}^-$ |
| :-----------: | :------: | :----: | :----: |
| $1\cdot 1 = 1$|   $1\cdot 0 = 0$   | $0\cdot 1 = 0$ | $0\cdot 0 = 0$ |
| $1\cdot 0 = 0$|   $1\cdot 1 = 1$   | $0\cdot 0 = 0$ | $0\cdot 1 = 0$ |
| $0\cdot 1 = 0$|  $0\cdot 0 = 0$   | $1\cdot 1 = 1$ | $1\cdot 0 = 0$ |
| $0\cdot 0 = 0$|  $0\cdot 1 = 0$   | $1\cdot 0 = 0$ | $1\cdot 1 = 1$ |

In other words, for each $k$, $\sum_{i=0}^{3}c_i(k)= N-k.$

Observe that
$$c_A(k) = (c_0(k) + c_3(k)) -(c_1(k) +c_2(k)) = N-k -2(c_1(k) +c_2(k)).$$

Therefore, for any $k$, the larger $|c_A(k)|$, the larger gap between $c_0(k) + c_3(k)$ (or $c_1(k) + c_2(k)$) and $(N-k)/2.$ Moreover,

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}c_A^2(k) = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(2(c_0(k) + c_3(k)) - (N-k))^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(N-k -2(c_1(k) + c_2(k)))^2.$$

## Global Automorphism Group in Golay's Merit Factor Problem

In Golay merit factor problem, the **global automorphism group** $G$ consists of transformations that leave the aperiodic autocorrelation energy - and thus the merit factor - invariant across the entire sequence space $\{-1, 1\}^N$, where $N$ is the length of binary sequences in the space.

For any fixed sequence length $N \ge 4$, $G$ has a strict **order of 8**.

---

### 1. Fundamental Generators
$G$ is generated by three basic operations applied to a binary sequence $\mathbf{x} = (x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{N-1})$:

1. **Negation ($f$):** $x_i \mapsto -x_i$
2. **Reversal ($g$):** $x_i \mapsto x_{N-1-i}$
3. **Alternation ($h$):** $x_i \mapsto (-1)^i x_i$

---

### 2. The 8 Group Elements
Combining the generators yields exactly 8 unique symmetry operations mapping coordinate $i$ for $i \in \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}$:

*   **$1$ (Identity):** $x_i \mapsto x_i$
*   **$f$ (Negation):** $x_i \mapsto -x_i$
*   **$g$ (Reversal):** $x_i \mapsto x_{N-1-i}$
*   **$fg$ (Negated Reversal):** $x_i \mapsto -x_{N-1-i}$
*   **$h$ (Alternation):** $x_i \mapsto (-1)^i x_i$
*   **$fh$ (Negated Alternation):** $x_i \mapsto (-1)^{i+1} x_i$
*   **$gh$ (Reversed Alternation):** $x_i \mapsto (-1)^i x_{N-1-i}$
*   **$fgh$ (Negated Reversed Alternation):** $x_i \mapsto (-1)^{i+1} x_{N-1-i}$

---

### 3. Algebraic Structure Dependance on Parity
While the mappings are the same, the algebraic structure depends completely on whether the sequence length $N$ is odd or even due to how Reversal ($g$) and Alternation ($h$) interact:

$$\text{Sign of } hg = (-1)^{N-1} \text{Sign of } gh$$

##### Case 1: Odd $N$ (Abelian)
*   **Property:** Reversal and alternation commute ($gh = hg$).
*   **Isomorphic Group:** Elementary Abelian Group **$\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$** (or $C_2^3$).
*   **Element Orders:** All 7 non-identity elements have an algebraic **order of 2**.

##### Case 2: Even $N$ (Non-Abelian)
*   **Property:** Reversal and alternation do not commute ($gh = fhg$).
*   **Isomorphic Group:** Dihedral Group of order 8 (**$D_4$**).
*   **Element Orders:**
    *   **Order 2:** $f, g, fg, h, fh$
    *   **Order 4:** $fg, fgh$ (where $(gh)^2 = f$ and $(gh)^4 = 1$)

---

### 4. Algorithmic Relevance (Search Space Reduction)
Because these 8 global automorphisms exhaustively preserve the merit factor for the entire space, exact solvers or heuristic algorithms can safely enforce symmetry-breaking constraints to yield a **factor-of-8 reduction** in the search grid.

## **Related Works**

- Currently, $6 \le \limsup_{n \to \infty}F_n$. More precisely, the value of $F_n$ has been computed in [Mertens and Bauke, 2004] for $N \le 60$ and large values of $F$ are known in [Knauer, 2004] for $61 \le N \le 271$.
- Conjecture (Hoholdt and Jensen, 1988) $\limsup_{N \to \infty}F_N = 6.$
- Conjecture (Littlewood, 1966) $\limsup_{N \to \infty}F_N = \infty.$
  * $\lim_{N \to \infty}F_N = \infty.$
  * $1/F_N = O(1/\sqrt{N})$ for infinitely many $N$.
  * $1/F_N = O(1/\sqrt{N})$ for every $N$.
- Conjecture (Borwein, Choi, and Jedwab, 2004) $\limsup_{N \to \infty}F_N > 6.34$ by numerical evidence from sequences up to millions of elements in length.
